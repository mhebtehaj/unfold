Hall, trees, and Morse matchings

From choosing distinct representatives to a space of compatible choices.

The common question is: can local choices fit together into one global object? Start with ordinary matching, add a condition that prevents cycles, then make a space whose faces record the choices that work together.

1 · Choosing representatives

Hall’s condition measures room to choose.

Suppose each set Si lists the representatives allowed for request i. We want one representative from each set, with no repetitions. Hall’s theorem says this is possible exactly when every collection I of requests has at least |I| available representatives altogether:

|⋃i∈I Si| ≥ |I|.

For example, three requests all restricted to {a,b} cannot be satisfied. The obstruction is visible before choosing anything: three requests have only two possible answers.

2 · Choosing edges

One extra vertex turns the condition into a tree theorem.

Now take n sets on n+1 vertices. Each Si specifies a complete graph: you may choose any pair of its vertices as an edge. Can you choose one edge per set so that the chosen edges form a spanning tree?

|⋃i∈I Si| ≥ |I| + 1   for every nonempty I.

This condition is necessary and sufficient. It is the dragon marriage theorem, a plausible match for the remembered theorem about selecting edges from complete graphs.

Source: Postnikov, Permutohedra, associahedra, and beyond, Remark 3.2 and Proposition 3.3.

Here the three sets are {a,b,c}, {b,c,d}, and {a,c,d}. A single set has three vertices; any two, or all three, cover four vertices. The condition holds. Try choosing ab, bc, cd, then compare with ab, bc, ac.

In the second choice, the edges close a triangle and leave d isolated. The theorem guarantees that a good selection exists; it does not say every selection works.

3 · Giving a forest a direction

A rooted forest is a Morse matching on a graph.

Choose a root in each tree. Pair every other vertex with the first edge on its path toward the root. No vertex or edge occurs in two pairs.

To check the Morse condition, draw the incidence diagram: its nodes are the graph’s vertices and edges. Direct incidences from edges down to vertices, then reverse the paired ones. A Morse matching requires this directed diagram to have no directed cycle. On a graph, these matchings correspond exactly to rooted forests.

Source: Chari and Joswig, Discrete Morse Complexes, Proposition 3.1. “Morse functions” here are identified when they induce the same matching.

Use the triangle boundary as the original graph. Pair a with ab and b with bc. Following arrows gives a → ab → b → bc → c. Add the third pair, c with ca, and the route closes back at a.

With just the first two pairs, c and the unused edge ca are critical, meaning unpaired. This is consistent with the original graph having one loop: the chosen tree contracts, but the extra edge remains.

4 · Making a space of choices

The matchings themselves form a topological object.

Make each possible vertex–edge pairing a new vertex. Declare a collection to be a face when its pairings together form a Morse matching. Removing pairs preserves validity, so this is a simplicial complex: the discrete Morse complex.

Source: Chari and Joswig, Section 2.

Restrict attention to A=(a,ab), B=(b,bc), and C=(c,ca). Each pair of choices works. All three together create the directed cycle above. Therefore the new space has three vertices and three edges, but no filled triangle.

This circle is the subcomplex on those three chosen pairings. The full discrete Morse complex also includes the other possible vertex–edge pairings.

5 · Returning to decision trees

Where this meets the ambiguity complex.

For a Boolean function f, an ambiguous partial assignment has completions giving both outputs. Its known literals form a face of the ambiguity complex Xf. Forgetting a literal preserves ambiguity, which explains the downward closure.

Take f(x)=x₁∨x₂∨x₃. Each single zero, and each pair of zeros, leaves the output undecided. Three zeros force output 0; any revealed one forces output 1. Thus Xf is a triangle boundary on the three zero literals.

A decision tree starts at the empty assignment. Each query offers two answer extensions. A branch stays inside Xf while uncertainty remains and leaves it when the output becomes determined. The all-zero branch grows from the empty face to a vertex, then an edge, then exits through the missing triangle.

The connection is real, but a correspondence still needs work. Morse paths alternate adding and removing faces. Query histories only add information, retain query labels, and must support both answers. A space of compatible local choices is a natural construction to investigate; identifying its faces with valid decision trees requires additional conditions. Its topology alone does not yet give a decision-tree cost bound.
